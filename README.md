# Promo codes: Why Most Fail at Checkout, Where the Working Ones Live, and How to Stack Them With Cashback

Search for "promo codes" and you'll get thousands of pages listing the same twelve codes under a "verified today" badge. Try them and most bounce back with "this code isn't valid." The codes aren't cursed. The system around them is doing what it was designed to do, and once you know the rules, you stop wasting twenty minutes per order on codes that were never going to work.

So this isn't another list that goes stale by Tuesday. It's the part that holds up: where working codes actually come from, why "invalid" is often the honest answer, and how to bolt a second discount onto your cart that doesn't evaporate the moment you find it.

## Why "invalid code" is usually telling the truth

Most broken codes fail for one of four reasons, and none of them are a website glitch.

**It was a campaign, not a fixture.** Codes get issued for a launch week, a single flash sale, or a sponsorship read. When the campaign ends, the code dies quietly. Aggregator sites rarely notice, because deleting a page costs them traffic. They leave it up, date-stamp it "verified today," and let you be the one who discovers the funeral.

**It's tied to your account state.** A lot of the codes floating around are first-order-only or new-customer-only. If you've bought from that merchant before, the code is working exactly as designed — you're just not the audience. Same story with codes that require a specific payment method or a specific app install.

**Your cart doesn't qualify.** Minimum spend thresholds, excluded brands, excluded sale items, gift cards and store credit left out of the calculation. A code that says "20% off" in the banner usually has three lines of exclusions underneath it that the banner cropped out.

**It came from the wrong place.** This one is subtle and worth knowing. Affiliate links work by dropping a tracking cookie or a tag on your session. If you arrive at a merchant through one site's link and then apply a code you found on a different site, you can overwrite the first attribution with the second. You still buy at the same price, but the party that was supposed to pay you back never gets credit for the sale. That's how a code can work perfectly and still cost you money.

## Where promo codes actually live

Ranked, roughly, by how often they turn into a real discount:

1. **The merchant's own checkout page.** Merchants surface live offers right where you pay, precisely because the code is guaranteed to be current. Ugly placement, highest hit rate.
2. **The merchant's newsletter or app signup.** A welcome code or a first-order discount in exchange for an email. Boring, reliable.
3. **Marketplace sale pages.** Platform-wide codes during 9.9, 11.11, Black Friday and similar windows. They expire hard, but they're real while they last.
4. **The cashback platform's merchant page.** This is the one people skip. Cashback sites list the merchant's own codes on the same page as the cashback rate, so the code and the tracking come from one place instead of two sites competing over your session. You get a working code plus a tracking link, not a code that may or may not have overwritten something else.
5. **Bank and card offers.** Usually not a typed code at all — cardholders get bonus rewards or a linked lounge. ShopBack runs a Westpac Lounge for Australian Westpac cardholders with bonus Cashback and weekly "Westpac Wednesdays" boosts, which stack on top of whatever the merchant is offering.
6. **Aggregator and coupon sites.** Free to check, just don't build your plan around them, and don't apply their codes to a cart you reached through another site's link.

## The stacking rule that makes this worth understanding

Discounts come from different budgets, so most of them don't cancel each other out. A typical order can carry four layers at once:

**Sale price + merchant promo code + cashback + card rewards.**

The sale price is the merchant's pricing decision. The code comes out of its promo budget. Cashback is funded from the affiliate commission the merchant pays to send you to its site — which is why your checkout total is identical whether you go through a cashback platform or type the URL in yourself. Card rewards are the bank's budget. Four separate pots, one cart.

Two caveats, both documented in the terms rather than hidden:

- A first purchase can't be stacked with other new-customer promotions. ShopBack's referral terms spell that out — if your friend's first order is riding on another "new customer" offer, the referral bonus doesn't pay.
- Your cashback tracks on the purchase amount, so a discount that reduces what you pay also reduces the commission the platform earns. That's normal, not a penalty.

The practical upside: cashback doesn't expire the way a code does. A code is a moment. Cashback sits in your account until the merchant's return window closes, and then it's yours to move out.

## Referral bonuses: read the conditions, not the headline number

Referral bonuses are the most misquoted part of any cashback platform, because the number in the ad is almost never the amount you can withdraw immediately.

ShopBack's own terms for Singapore lay out the chain: your friend has to sign up through your link or code. You earn the bonus once they make a valid purchase that meets the minimum spend and update their withdrawal details. To unlock the bonus you need that qualifying purchase **and** withdrawal details set up within 180 days of signup. And to actually withdraw a referral bonus, you need a separate balance of confirmed cashback from your own shopping that isn't itself a referral bonus. There's also a list of ways to lose the bonus that reads like an anti-spam policy: self-referral, a referred friend signing up on a device you've already used, posting your link on a merchant's Facebook page or forum, disguising a referral link as a generic link, or buying ads on branded keywords. Orders that are cancelled, exchanged or refunded don't count toward minimum spend. None of this is unusual for a rewards programme; it's just the part the ads leave out.

## Four ways people lose cashback they already earned

- **Starting at the merchant instead of the platform.** The single most common cause. Each purchase needs its own click-through, every time, no exceptions for stores you've used before.
- **Running an ad blocker or switching browser mid-session.** These can break the tracking that ties your order to your account.
- **Returning or cancelling part of the order.** Refunded items don't earn cashback, and the amount adjusts accordingly.
- **Stacking a first order with another new-customer promotion.** Documents as a disqualifier in referral terms, and it can void the referral bonus for both parties.

And yes, sometimes tracking fails for no obvious reason. A German consumer test found their cashback simply absent from the account, and it only appeared after they contacted support directly. That's the case for checking your pending balance within a few days of a purchase rather than assuming everything worked.
